## A widely misread phenomenon

Japanese petrochemicals have long relied on industry voluntary action plans rather than mandatory carbon pricing. From outside, this reads as weak policy. Execution data suggests otherwise: the 18 sites in our sample reached 87% of their voluntary targets, above the comparable performance of some regimes that converted to mandatory rules.

This report does not argue that voluntary mechanisms are superior. It identifies the conditions that let this one work — and how scarce those conditions are.

## Three supporting conditions

**High industry concentration.** A small number of corporate groups dominate, keeping coordination costs low and making the reputational cost of defection high. Where industry structure is fragmented, the same mechanism lacks enforcement.

**Long-term employment and retained technical knowledge.** The process knowledge required for equipment replacement stays inside firms, insulating long-horizon investment from short-term financial pressure.

**Subsidy and demonstration support.** Funding under the GX Promotion Act reduces the first-mover cost disadvantage, making voluntary commitments commercially viable. Without this layer, voluntary schemes degrade into declarations.

## Why it does not transfer

Taiwan's sector is similarly concentrated but lacks the coordinating traditions and reputational machinery of Japanese industry associations. The UK sector is highly internationalised, with decision centres outside the country and correspondingly weak local association authority.

**The value of institutional comparison lies in identifying conditions, not in selecting models.** Our main conclusion is that the transferable element of the Japanese case is the subsidy and demonstration design, not the voluntary mechanism itself.

## Open question

Carbon pricing timelines remain under revision. Whether the existing voluntary regime is replaced or runs alongside a mandatory price is the focus of our continued tracking.
