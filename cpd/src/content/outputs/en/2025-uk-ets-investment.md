## Design

We track 37 major equipment investment decisions across nine UK petrochemical sites from 2013 to 2024, matched against the prevailing carbon price at the point of decision, free allocation status, and the internal carbon price assumptions firms disclose in annual reports and investor presentations.

## Finding one: the effect has a threshold and a lag

Below roughly £30 per tonne, investment decisions show almost no response. Above £50, acceleration of equipment replacement becomes visible — with an average lag of 3.4 years from price movement to capital expenditure decision.

That lag matters for evaluation. Studies matching annual carbon prices against annual emissions changes will understate the effect of pricing and misattribute the subsequent reduction to whatever other measures were in force at the time.

## Finding two: part of the reduction is displacement

Absolute emissions from the UK sector fell 23.1% over the period, the steepest of the three countries. Between 6 and 9 percentage points of that corresponds to offshoring — closed lines reappearing as new capacity in continental Europe and Asia.

We cannot attribute each closure precisely, and carbon cost is one factor among many. What is certain is that **territorial emissions accounting records this as successful reduction**. Where the purpose of comparison is to assess a policy's contribution to global emissions, that treatment is not appropriate.

## Implication for comparison

The UK case shows carbon pricing works, and also shows its effect is overstated by territorial accounting. This must be netted out explicitly when comparing against Taiwan, which lacks a mandatory price, and Japan, which relies on voluntary mechanisms.
